How far away is fascism in Taiwan? When disagreement starts to look like betrayal

When people hear the word "fascism", many think first of Hitler, Mussolini, the Nazis, uniforms and the Second World War.

But fascism is more than a period of history. It is also a set of political ideas about the nation, power, leaders and the people. Understanding its features still matters for democratic societies today.

This is especially true for Taiwan. We lived through a long period of authoritarian rule, and today's democratic system took shape only a few decades ago. We have deep historical experience of political loyalty, censorship and the way dissent is treated.

Before going further, one thing needs to be clear. Not all authoritarian politics is fascism. A forceful politician, or a party that pushes out dissenters, does not automatically earn the label.

If we want to use history to understand today's politics, we need to be all the more careful about what the features of fascism actually are.

An axe and a bundle of rods

The word fascism comes from the Italian fascismo, and its roots go back to the fasces of ancient Rome.

The fasces were a bundle of rods tied together, sometimes with an axe set among them. They stood for the authority of magistrates and the state's power to punish.

Later, the Italian Fascist movement took up this symbol and amplified its meaning of unity and collective strength.

A single rod is easy to break. A bundle of rods tied together is not.

Unity is strength. That hardly sounds like a problem.

But the problem lies here: Who decides that we should be tied together? Who has the right to decide who belongs in the bundle? And what happens to someone who does not wish to be tied?

These questions are a good starting point for understanding fascism.

We were once great, and now we are in decline

Historians of fascism disagree about how to define it, but one core idea keeps coming back: the decline and rebirth of the nation.

Fascism often begins with a very compelling story.

Our country was once great. Now it has grown weak, society is in disorder, traditional values are gone, and we have lost the place that should be ours.

Then it offers an explanation: all this happened because certain people betrayed the nation, because outside forces are undermining us, or because democracy is too soft to let the country act.

Finally it offers a remedy: we must unite again, we need strong leadership, and we must clear away whatever stands in the way of national revival.

The great appeal of this story is that it offers an emotional outlet as well as a political programme.

When people feel frustrated about the economy, society or politics, it tells them: your discontent has a cause, your anger is justified, and we know who the enemy is.

The historian Roger Griffin summed up the core of fascist thought as palingenetic ultranationalism, an extreme nationalism built on the idea of national rebirth.

Put simply, it is the belief that the nation is in decline and that a thorough political and social transformation must bring about its rebirth and build a new order.

Patriotism, national identity and the wish for a better country are not fascism in themselves.

What really marks it out is the point at which the ideal of national rebirth joins with an exclusive idea of who counts as a member of the nation, authoritarian rule, the removal of internal enemies and the rejection of liberal democracy.

How do historians recognise fascism?

The historian Stanley G. Payne proposed a framework for analysing fascism that can be read from three angles: what it opposes, what it wants to build, and how it tends to reach its goals.

First, what it opposes.

Fascism opposes liberalism. Liberalism values individual rights, political pluralism and checks on power, while fascism holds that individual freedom must not get in the way of the nation's collective goals.

It also opposes communism, and in particular class struggle and internationalism as the core of politics. It argues that all social classes should serve the common interest of the nation.

More surprisingly, it opposes traditional conservatism as well.

Traditional conservatism usually values existing institutions, social order and gradual change. Fascism has revolutionary aims. It sees the old political system as corrupt and weak, and wants to build a new national order through intense political mobilisation and social transformation.

Of course, fascist movements in history often worked with conservatives, so the two should not be seen as complete opposites.

Second, what it wants to build.

Fascism seeks a highly unified, powerful nation-state that can act quickly. Political power is concentrated in the leader and the party, economic and social organisations must serve national goals, and the people are mobilised for national revival. Historically, fascist movements have also often been accompanied by ambitions of foreign expansion and empire.

More notably, Payne also includes a positive attitude towards violence and war. Fascist movements may even portray violence as a necessary means of removing enemies, restoring order and achieving national rebirth.

Finally, its political style.

Fascism puts great weight on mass mobilisation, fiery speeches, flags and uniforms, and a political aesthetic of strength, discipline, sacrifice and heroism.

It tends to build up a charismatic leader, leading people to believe that only this person can bring the country out of crisis.

Taken together, these three angles make the core of fascist thought easier to grasp:

It holds that the nation is in decline, that existing democratic institutions and traditional political forces can no longer save it, and that a strong leader must therefore mobilise the whole nation, remove what stands in the way of revival and build an entirely new political order.

This also explains why fascism is hard to understand through conventional left and right policies. It is usually classed as far right in its political thought, yet it also has radical ambitions for social transformation.

So to judge whether a political movement is fascist, it is not enough to ask whether it is patriotic, values order or has a strong leader. It is not even enough to ask whether it suppresses opponents.

We need to see whether these features together form a body of ideas and a political movement that seeks national rebirth, rejects political pluralism, supports authoritarian rule and justifies political violence.

When the leader becomes the embodiment of the nation

In a democracy, politicians are people entrusted by the public to hold power for a time. They can be supported and criticised. They can win elections and lose power.

In a fascist political culture, the leader is often cast as the voice of the national will and the saviour of the nation's destiny.

So criticising the leader can be read as a lack of patriotism, and opposing his policies can be seen as holding the country back.

Political loyalty then begins to move away from institutions and the constitution and towards one particular person.

For people in Taiwan, the cult of the leader is not an unfamiliar historical phenomenon. In the authoritarian years, the image of the political leader reached deep into schools, public spaces and daily life. Loyalty to the leader and loyalty to the state were often tied together.

A distinction is still needed here. The cult of the leader is not unique to fascism. Other kinds of authoritarian regime can show something similar.

What deserves real concern is when the leader is seen as the sole representative of the nation and its will, and people gradually come to accept an idea: as long as the leader is acting for the good of the country, breaking procedures and damaging institutions can be forgiven.

This is also why democracy needs checks on power.

Democracy is sometimes slow. Policies need discussion, laws need deliberation, governments need oversight, and differing views need negotiation.

But these procedures exist precisely because we cannot assume that those in power are always right.

When a government begins to believe that enough public support frees it from institutional constraints, democracy may start to erode from within.

When disagreement becomes betrayal

To me, this is where fascism and modern politics in Taiwan are most worth discussing together.

Within a political party, it is quite normal for some people to support the leader and others to criticise the leader.

After all, sharing political ideals does not mean holding the same view on every policy and every decision.

But when a party can no longer tolerate criticism, treats people with different views as disloyal, or even tries to drive them out, there is reason for concern.

The same can happen among supporters.

When someone raises a different view, the first reaction is not to discuss whether the point has merit, but to rush to apply a label:

"Are you with the other party?"

"Are you speaking for the enemy?"

"How could a real supporter say something like that?"

Behind these words lies a dangerous political logic:

Whether a person's opinion deserves to be heard depends on which camp that person is loyal to.

In Taiwan, political discussion often revolves around party identities such as blue, green and white. Sometimes a person who merely questions one policy is taken for a supporter of another camp.

But a person can fully support a party's general direction while opposing one of its policies. A person can also reject another party's ideas while approving of some of the specific things it has done.

If people must accept every decision of the party they support or else have their loyalty questioned, political support can easily turn into obedience.

Of course, parties have their own principles and discipline, and breaches of party rules may have reasonable grounds for sanction. A party expelling a member, or people throwing political labels around online, does not by itself make something fascist.

These behaviours relate more directly to anti-pluralism, internal party democracy and an authoritarian political culture.

Only when they are further combined with exclusive ultranationalism, the political goal of national rebirth, the rejection of democratic institutions and the justification of political violence do they come closer to the fascism historians discuss.

Does a political group treat different views as a chance to improve itself, or as a threat to be removed?

This question may deserve closer attention than its public political performance.

When Taiwan did not allow dissent

For Taiwan, these questions are more than abstract political theory.

From 1949 to 1987, the main island of Taiwan lived under martial law for 38 years (Kinmen and Matsu were released from it only in 1992).

In those years, freedom of speech, publication, assembly and party formation was tightly restricted. Political dissidents could be accused of being "Communist spies" or "rebels", or of endangering national security.

These accusations were not aimed only at people actually involved in Communist organisations. Those who advocated Taiwan independence, pushed for democratic reform or simply criticised the authorities could all become targets of political persecution.

The Lei Chen case of 1960 is an example worth recalling.

Lei Chen had close ties to Kuomintang political circles and was one of the founders of the magazine Free China. He was firmly anti-Communist in his early years, but as his criticism of the government grew stronger, his party membership was cancelled in 1954.

In 1960 he went further and took part in preparing an opposition party. He was then arrested and sentenced to ten years in prison on charges related to Communist spies and rebellion.

What stands out in this history is that even someone with close ties to the ruling system, and who shared its anti-Communist stance, could be crushed by the state machine for advocating democratic reform and challenging political power.

Even so, the Lei Chen case mainly shows how authoritarian rule suppressed political dissent, and how the state limited people's freedom in the name of security and political loyalty.

It is not enough to prove that the regime in Taiwan at that time was fascist.

In fact, the historical relationship between the Kuomintang and fascism is more complicated than that.

In the 1930s, Chiang Kai-shek and some political organisations within the Kuomintang were indeed influenced by the fascism of the time.

One example is the Blue Shirts and related secret political groups close to Chiang, which stressed loyalty to the leader, national revival, discipline, anti-Communism and political mobilisation. The New Life Movement launched in 1934 also tried to reshape citizens' daily behaviour through discipline and moral rules.

Historians have studied these phenomena in the context of fascism.

But whether the party-state authoritarian system in Taiwan after 1949 should be classed as a fascist regime as a whole still requires distinguishing its political goals, organisational forms and methods of rule.

Some Kuomintang factions in the 1930s had radical nationalist ambitions and aims of social transformation. After the move to Taiwan, the Kuomintang regime gradually took the form of authoritarian rule marked by anti-Communism, regime maintenance, intelligence control and the party-state system.

The two periods show continuity, and also important differences.

So we do not need to call all authoritarian rule fascism in order to take seriously the harm it did to freedom and human rights. Equally, the differences should not lead us to ignore the fascist ideas and influences that really existed in history.

Taiwan today is no longer in that era.

We can openly criticise the president, form opposition parties, change the government through elections and express all kinds of political views online.

These freedoms were gained gradually, through Taiwan's long process of democratisation.

Facing an external threat, how does a democracy keep its principles?

Taiwan is in a particular situation: we do face military threats, political pressure and the risk of information operations from China.

Any discussion of Taiwan's democracy and national security therefore cannot ignore this real external threat.

Some argue for stronger defence and deterrence, some stress exchange and dialogue, and some think both must go together. Behind these positions may lie different risk assessments, political ideas and even national identities.

But whichever strategy one favours, one must take the threat itself seriously and explain how one's position would protect Taiwan's security.

Democracy needs oversight. Defence budgets, military procurement and security policy should all face reasonable scrutiny, to avoid waste, abuse of power and wrong decisions.

But oversight must also be responsible.

If, in the name of oversight, one can never offer reasonable questions or concrete alternatives, or fails to account for gaps in defence that may result, that political behaviour also deserves strict examination.

Of course, opposition to a defence policy, or delay from budget review, does not by itself show that anyone is deliberately harming national security. Whether something amounts to deliberate obstruction still requires concrete evidence.

Equally, an external threat does not justify asking everyone to give up oversight and letting those in power hold unchecked authority.

Democratic defence requires two equally important responsibilities: those in power must accept oversight, and those who oversee must answer for their decisions and the possible consequences.

It also needs saying that different judgements about external threats, disputes over defence policy, and even irresponsible political obstruction cannot simply be treated as fascism.

These problems can be examined in terms of policy effects, political responsibility, national security and democratic institutions.

The test for fascism must still return to its specific ideological core and political goals.

For Taiwan, what really deserves continued discussion is how to build a democratic system that can both protect national security effectively and maintain checks on power and political accountability.

How, then, should we recognise fascism?

At this point, I think the discussion so far can be gathered into a clearer way of judging.

When we see a politician or political group show forceful leadership, push out dissenters, demand loyalty or even try to weaken institutional checks, we have good reason to ask whether it has authoritarian tendencies.

But to go further and speak of fascism, a few more questions need asking.

Does it claim that the nation is in decline and can be reborn only through thorough political transformation?

Does it hold that only certain people are true members of the nation, and that others are obstacles to national revival?

Does it try to replace the pluralist competition and checks on power of a democracy with a single leader and political movement?

Does it treat political violence, internal purges or foreign aggression as legitimate means of achieving national goals?

These questions cannot simply be used as a checklist, and there is no formula under which a certain number of ticks makes something fascist.

But they can help us distinguish ordinary political conflict, authoritarian tendencies, and fascist movements with a specific ideology and political goals.

In Taiwan especially, when we use the word "fascist" to criticise contemporary politics, we should be clear: are we pointing to a particular anti-democratic act, or claiming that a political movement has the overall features of fascism?

The two are not the same.

In the end, what should we watch for?

To me, the most important point of understanding fascism is to learn to recognise a dangerous political logic, while avoiding reducing complicated political problems to a label.

It does not necessarily begin with cancelling elections, or with asking people to give up their freedom from the start.

It may begin with a wish that is easy to share: a better country, a united society, more effective politics, respect for one's own culture.

There is nothing wrong with these wishes.

The real turning point comes when national revival is used to justify excluding others, concentrating power, rejecting democratic institutions or even using violence.

Let us return to that bundle of rods from ancient Rome.

Unity can indeed bring strength.

But the unity of a free society should rest on different people being willing to live together and respect one another, not on asking everyone to give up their differences and obey a single will.

Taiwan's democracy was hard won. I hope that what we cherish is not only the chance for the party we support to win elections, but also the right of those who disagree with us to speak, take part in politics and be treated fairly.

Perhaps the most precious thing about democracy is that we do not need to think alike in order to live together in this country.

We can hold different positions, argue fiercely and stand by our beliefs, without having to become one another's enemies.

Understanding fascism may also be about more than identifying political enemies.

The more important question is this: when the politicians we support begin to push out dissenters, demand absolute loyalty or even try to free their power from checks, are we still willing to raise questions?

If the same thing happened in the camp we support, would we point out the problem?
